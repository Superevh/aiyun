Most people do not hate icebreakers. They hate bad ones: the forced "share a fun fact" round, the game that runs twenty minutes too long, the question that makes someone reveal more than they wanted to. A good icebreaker is short, optional to go deep on, and actually a bit fun.

This guide gives you icebreaker games for remote teams that work on a video call, sorted by how much time you have, plus a few rules that keep them from feeling awkward.

What makes a remote icebreaker work

A remote icebreaker works when it is short, fair to everyone on the call, low-stakes to share, clearly connected to the meeting's purpose, and easy to pass on. For a regular meeting, five minutes is plenty; save longer games for team socials and offsites.

  • It is short. For a regular meeting, five minutes is plenty. Save longer games for socials and offsites.
  • Everyone can play at the same level. Avoid games that reward native English speakers, extroverts or people with nice home offices.
  • Sharing is low-stakes. People choose how personal to get. "What did you have for lunch?" is safer than "What is your biggest fear?".
  • It has a point. Warming people up before a retro or workshop is a good point. "We always do one" is not.
  • Passing is fine. Say so out loud at the start.

No-tool icebreakers

Sometimes you just want something you can start with zero setup. These need only a video call.

One-word check-in

Everyone describes how they are arriving today in one word. Takes two minutes for a team of eight, and often tells you more than a long status update.

Weather report

"If your week so far was weather, what would it be?" Sunny, overcast, a southerly buster. It lets people signal a rough week without having to explain it.

Show something within reach

Everyone grabs one object within arm's length and says a sentence about it. Mugs, pets and odd desk toys dominate. Great for new teams.

This or that

Quick-fire choices: beach or bush, tea or coffee, early bird or night owl. People answer in the chat, which keeps it fast.

Rose and thorn

One good thing and one hard thing from the week. A nice warm-up for a retro, because it gets people reflecting.

Show your view

For a team spread across cities, have everyone briefly point their camera out the window or describe their view. Hobart rain and Darwin heat in the same meeting is a good reminder that you are one team in very different places.

Tiny questions

Pick one light question and go round quickly:

  • What is something you are looking forward to this month?
  • What was your first job?
  • What is a small thing that made your week better?
  • What is a skill you would like to learn?
  • What is the best thing you have eaten recently?

What to pick by meeting length

Match the icebreaker to the meeting: a minute at most, and only occasionally, for a stand-up; three to five minutes for a weekly team meeting; about five minutes for a sprint retro; ten to fifteen minutes for a workshop; and a whole session of games for a team social.

Virtual team building games: how to run a 45-minute social

If you are planning a dedicated session, a simple structure keeps energy up. For more options by group size, see virtual team building games.

  1. 0–5 min: people arrive, music on, a warm-up like Hot Take Slider while you wait for stragglers.
  2. 5–20 min: one main game with lots of interaction, such as Draw & Guess.
  3. 20–35 min: a quieter game where people learn something about each other, such as Who Wrote It? or Two Truths & a Lie.
  4. 35–45 min: a fast, silly closer like Emoji Charades, then wrap up on time.

End on time even if people are enjoying it. Finishing while energy is high is what makes people come back next time.

Tips from facilitating remote teams

  • Go first yourself. Model the level of sharing you want: light, honest, a little self-deprecating.
  • Use the chat. Chat answers are faster and less exposing than talking one at a time.
  • Mind time zones. If the session is at 4pm in Perth it is 6pm or 7pm in Sydney or Melbourne. Socials should happen in working hours for everyone, or rotate the slot.
  • Think about language. For multilingual teams, visual games like Zoom In or Emoji Charades are fairer than word-heavy ones.
  • Never force cameras. Invite them, do not require them.
  • Read the room. If the team is under deadline pressure or something hard has happened, skip the game and do a simple check-in instead.
  • Rotate who picks. Let a different person choose the icebreaker each week. People enjoy games more when a peer chose them.

Icebreakers to avoid

  • Anything that asks about family, health, money or religion.
  • "Share an embarrassing story". Some people will hate it, and the ones who do will not tell you.
  • Games with a clear loser who gets singled out.
  • Anything that needs everyone to install software or create an account.
  • Long rounds where each person talks for two minutes in a 15-person meeting. That is half an hour.

Frequently asked questions

How often should a remote team do icebreakers?

A short one at the start of retros and workshops, and a proper team game every month or so, works for most teams. Daily is usually too much.

What is the best icebreaker for a large group?

Anything where people answer in parallel rather than one at a time: chat-based "this or that", Hot Take Slider, or Higher or Lower. Going round 30 people takes too long.

How long should an icebreaker take?

For a regular meeting, three to five minutes is plenty, and a stand-up needs a minute at most, if any. Workshops can spend ten to fifteen minutes, and a team social can be games for the whole session.
