25 sprint retrospective ideas and formats (with when to use each)

25 sprint retrospective ideas and retro formats, with a table showing what each is for and when to use it, plus facilitation tips and how to make actions stick.

First, the shape every good retro follows

Every good sprint retrospective follows the same five steps, described by Esther Derby and Diana Larsen in Agile Retrospectives (2006): set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, and close. Most retro formats are just different ways to run the middle of that structure.

  1. Set the stage – remind people why you are here and make it safe to speak.
  2. Gather data – what actually happened this sprint.
  3. Generate insights – why it happened.
  4. Decide what to do – one to three concrete actions.
  5. Close – a quick check on whether the retro itself was worth the time.

Many teams open by reading Norm Kerth's Prime Directive, which asks everyone to assume people did the best job they could with what they knew at the time. It sounds formal, but it does a useful job: it moves the conversation from blame to systems.

How to pick: match the format to the moment

The team is new, or retros are new

Stick to plain formats: Start, Stop, Continue or Went well / To improve. People need to learn that the retro is safe and that actions happen before they need clever metaphors.

Something went wrong

Use a Timeline to rebuild what happened, then Five whys on the one or two moments that mattered most. Keep the Prime Directive visible. If the issue was a production incident, a separate blameless post-incident review is usually better than squeezing it into the sprint retro.

Morale is low

Mad, Sad, Glad lets feelings out in a structured way. Follow it with a kudos round so people leave lighter than they arrived. If the same frustrations keep showing up, Circles and soup helps the team separate what they can fix from what they need to escalate.

Things are fine, which is its own problem

Comfortable teams drift. Starfish or KALM ask for small adjustments rather than big problems. A Pre-mortem on the next piece of work often surfaces risks nobody mentions in a "how did it go" retro.

You are short on time

Run the open one-question retro in 15 minutes: two minutes silent writing, group, vote, discuss the top item, agree one action. Done is better than skipped.

Facilitation tips that make any format work

  • Write silently first. Give everyone three to five minutes to add notes before anyone talks. It stops the loudest person setting the agenda.
  • Let people be anonymous when it matters. For sensitive topics, anonymous cards get you the honest version. You can reveal authors later if the team is comfortable.
  • Group, then vote. Cluster similar cards, then give each person three votes. Talk about the top two or three clusters only.
  • Timebox the discussion. Eight to ten minutes per topic is plenty. If it needs more, it probably needs its own meeting.
  • Rotate the facilitator. When the Scrum Master always runs it, the retro starts to belong to them. Rotating spreads ownership.
  • Remote? Turn cameras on for the discussion, not the writing. Silent writing works fine with cameras off; discussion is better when people can see each other.
  • Mind the time zones. A team spread across Perth and Sydney has a two or three hour gap, depending on daylight saving. Do not always make the same people stay late.

How to make action items actually stick

To make retro action items stick, agree only one to three small, specific actions, give each a named owner, put them on the sprint board with the rest of the work, and open the next retro by reviewing them. The most common complaint about retros is not the format; it is that nothing changes.

  1. Fewer actions. One to three per retro. Ten actions means zero actions.
  2. Make them small and specific. "Improve communication" is a wish. "Post a daily update in the team channel by 10am, starting Monday" is an action.
  3. One owner each. A named person, not "the team". They do not have to do all the work; they make sure it happens.
  4. Put them where the work lives. Add actions to the sprint backlog or board, so they compete for time like any other work.
  5. Open the next retro with them. Spend the first five minutes on last sprint's actions: done, in progress, or dropped on purpose. This single habit does more than any format.
  6. Drop actions honestly. If an action no longer matters, close it and say so. Zombie actions teach people that retro actions are not real.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a sprint retrospective take?

The Scrum Guide caps the retrospective at three hours for a one-month sprint and says it is usually shorter for shorter sprints. For a two-week sprint, 45 to 60 minutes works for most teams.

How often should you change the retrospective format?

Every two to four sprints is a good rhythm. Change it sooner if the retro feels flat or the team's situation changes.

Should managers attend sprint retrospectives?

Ask the team. If people speak less when a manager is in the room, run the retro without them and share the actions afterwards.

What is the best retrospective format for a new team?

Start with a plain format such as Start, Stop, Continue or Went well / To improve. New teams first need to learn that the retro is safe and that agreed actions actually happen.
